An exemplar of AI for disease diagnosis

My section of AI in Healthcare: Disease Diagnoses, Autonomous Workflows and Consumer LLMs, a paper written with Ra’Kira Nelson, Cynthia Ma and Amy Chen. Only the part I wrote is reproduced here; references are renumbered for this excerpt.

Introduction

Around 56,000 Americans die every year from colon cancer, with one in twenty developing the disease [1]. One exemplary demonstration of how AI can be applied to healthcare is a groundbreaking paper that introduced a new computer vision technique to detect cancerous polyps in the colon with high accuracy. In this section, we will explain how this technique works and the decisions behind its design.

How it works

To generate the data, a virtual colonoscopy is conducted via a computed tomography (CT) scan [1], in which an X-ray scanner rotates around the patient to construct a 3D view of the colon.

To find candidates for polyps in the colon, we look for surfaces which are spherical in shape. If the inward normal vectors for the surfaces intersect around a single point, that is, the center of a sphere, then the surface must be approximately spherical, and is considered a polyp candidate. This technique, which identifies shapes by the intersection of normal vectors, is known as the Hough Transform [1].

Previously, this was the best way to look for polyps, but the issue was that any spherical shape would be flagged. Many of these were not polyps, but merely normal tissue. This high rate of false positives meant that radiologists spent lots of time looking through the candidates to determine what actually is a polyp. So the contribution of the paper is the pipeline that follows which effectively classifies the polyps among these candidates.

The key technique of the paper is inspired by how radiologists view the 3D scans of polyp candidates [1]. They use three perspectives: the axial, sagittal, and coronal planes, i.e. from the top, side, and front. Each of these planes are orthogonal to the others to provide a fuller view of the candidate shape. This paper uses the same concept, but takes many such triples of cross-sections, with triple oriented in a random rotation to account for any orientation of the candidate. One hundred of these triples of perpendicular "slices" are taken per candidate. That is the namesake of the technique, Random Orthogonal Shape Sections (ROSS) [1].

These cross-sectional images are matched against simple shapes such as circles, ellipsoids, and quadratics to see how well they fit. The concept is that knowing how accurately different shapes match the image can give us an idea of how the image looks [1].

These measurements are used to populate a histogram, which acts as a "signature" for the image [1]. Polyps and normal tissue tend to have different looking histograms, so they will be used as input for the classification model.

The paper uses a standard technique known as a Support Vector Machine (SVM) to classify the polyps [1]. This technique projects data points into higher-dimensional space with a function known as a kernel, then separates the data with a hyperplane. This acts as the decision boundary, so points are classified as polyps or normal based on which side of the hyperplane they are on. The data and the hyperplane are projected back down into the original dimension to create a nonlinear decision boundary.

This pipeline had groundbreaking results, and was able to detect polyps while flagging much fewer normal tissues. At a sensitivity of 1.00, which means that every polyp is flagged, the percentage of normal tissue classified as normal increased from 19% to 69% [1]. While radiologists previously had to deal with lots of normal tissue incorrectly flagged as polyps, now they can save time because the candidates are much more likely to be actual polyps. This speeds up the diagnostic process and benefits both the radiologists and the patients.

Transparency

One key advantage of this paper is that it uses Support Vector Machines. This is a traditional AI technique which is very interpretable, because it reveals which inputs are used to mark the decision boundary. Known as the "support vectors", they represent the data points which happen to be nearest the decision boundary. In our case, the support vectors would be the histograms of the polyp candidates which influence where the line is drawn between normal tissue and cancerous polyps.

This transparency is very useful for analyzing the model and understanding how it makes its decisions. Researchers can tweak the model if something looks off, and radiologists can learn about the features of a candidate that sits near the border of normal and cancerous. The accuracy of both the AI and human diagnoses can be improved in this way.

Transparency is a fundamental issue of modern AI that we discuss in detail. So, this paper is an exemplar of how AI can be both effective and transparent, and illustrates the desirable benefits that transparency brings.
